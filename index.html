FIELD NOTES · DATA JOURNALISM REMAKE

THE DUNE NETWORK

Rebuilding and critiquing a Reuters graphic on how one tanker moved sanctioned oil through 33 transfers with 11 ships

SOURCE: REUTERS GRAPHICS, "HOW IRAN MOVES SANCTIONED OIL AROUND THE WORLD" · REMAKE TOOLING: P5.JS

Iran's oil sector has been squeezed by layers of U.S., EU, and UN sanctions for over a decade, yet crude remains the country's single biggest source of foreign currency. To keep it flowing, brokers rely on a well-documented workaround: ship-to-ship (STS) transfers conducted quietly at sea, which blend cargo and paperwork until the oil's origin becomes very hard to trace.

Reuters' investigation centered on one vessel, the tanker Dune, which the reporting ties to roughly 33 separate transfers with 11 other ships, moving on the order of 5 million barrels of crude. Their graphic renders this as a Sankey-style diagram: a dark canvas, glowing gold lines, Dune at the center, thickness encoding volume.

How the transfer works

A ship-to-ship transfer is simple in principle: two tankers meet at sea, tie up alongside one another, and pump crude from one hull to the other. What makes it a sanctions workaround is everything around that act. A vessel preparing to load sanctioned crude will often switch off its Automatic Identification System (AIS) transponder beforehand, so the meeting itself never appears on public tracking services. Once the oil has changed vessels, it can be re-papered with new documentation, sometimes listing a different country of origin, before continuing on to a buyer.

None of this requires new technology — aging tankers, a quiet stretch of water, and a willingness to operate without insurance from Western underwriters are enough. That's part of why enforcement is difficult: the "shadow fleet" doing this work is large, constantly repainted and renamed, and spread across international waters where no single authority has full jurisdiction.

The vessel at the center

Dune is identified in sanctions-related reporting and U.S. Treasury designations as a tanker linked to Iran's National Iranian Tanker Company (NITC) network — the state-linked fleet the U.S. has repeatedly targeted for its role moving Iranian crude. Treasury actions describe transfers from NITC-linked vessels to buyers via intermediary companies, with cargo eventually blended into the broader international market. Dune's reported transfer count — dozens of handoffs with a wide set of partner vessels — is consistent with a ship functioning as a hub rather than a one-off participant.

That's a striking piece of design — but a Sankey diagram usually encodes sequential stages (A → B → C). What's actually being described here is closer to one hub trading repeatedly with many spokes over time. Below is a rebuild that keeps the volume-as-thickness idea, but structures it as a radial hub network instead — plus notes on where that change helps, and where it might lose something the original had.

What changed, and why

Original approach

  • Sankey-style layout implies staged, directional flow
  • Dark background + glow prioritizes drama and cinematic reveal
  • Ship icons carry narrative texture (this is a specific vessel, not a data point)
  • Static image — thickness must be judged by eye alone

This remake

  • Radial hub layout — matches the actual "one hub, many spokes" structure
  • Same dark/gold palette, kept because it genuinely suits the "shadow fleet" subject
  • Hover interaction surfaces exact reconstructed numbers instead of relying on line width alone
  • Sortable by volume vs. transfer count, so a small-volume-but-frequent partner isn't hidden by a large single line

Net take: the original's Sankey look is more visually authoritative at a glance, but it slightly overstates structure the data doesn't have (there's no real "stage two"). The radial layout is more honest about the shape of the relationship, at the cost of some of the cinematic punch that made the original memorable.
